Why the dead companies matter more than the live ones

If you test a strategy only on companies that still exist today, you have already told it the answer. Every stock in that list survived. The strategy looks brilliant because it was never given the chance to buy Nortel in 2000, WorldCom in 2001, or Silicon Graphics on the way down — and a real investor, choosing in real time, absolutely would have.

That flattery has a name: survivorship bias. It is the single most common reason a backtest that looks wonderful makes no money. The 39 companies below are here so it cannot happen. A strategy that buys strength has to buy them too, at their peaks, and ride them down.

Rules the roster obeys

What keeps it honest
Applied on every single day of the test
  • Nothing before it existed. A company enters only after its IPO plus six months of trading history. No buying Arm in 2019.
  • Nothing after it died. A delisted name is sold at its last price and never traded again — but its earlier years stay fully in play.
  • Nothing untradeable. Names under $3, or without enough daily volume to get in and out of, are skipped on the days that is true of them.
  • Names are not identities. Every company has a permanent internal ID. Broadcom is the clearest trap: Avago bought the Broadcom name in 2016, so a price series labelled “Broadcom” starts in 2009 and hides the original company's dot-com boom and collapse entirely. Here they are two separate companies and are never joined.
What is still missing
Honest limitations of this list
  • Real prices. Still the big one. The roster is defined; the download has not run.
  • Free data may not have the dead names. Free providers often serve no history at all for delisted tickers. If that happens, the study reports itself as survivorship-limited rather than pretending the list above was honoured.
  • It is not the whole market. 81 live names is a considered selection, not every listed technology company. Anything excluded is excluded by the list above, which is at least visible and arguable.
  • Delisting prices are optimistic. A bankruptcy is modelled as a sale at the last quoted price. In reality the exit is usually worse.
